What you buy says more than who you are

The survey asks each respondent how many people live in their household. The purchase log records what that same account bought, when, and for how much. Purchase behavior predicts household size; demographics barely move the prediction.

Question and data

Five thousand respondents, one capped target

The survey and the purchase log are joined on a single respondent ID. The target is self-reported household size, which the survey caps at "4 or more".

5,027

Survey respondents, each already matched to a purchase history

3,027

Rows with a household size. These are the only rows that can be scored.

2,000

Rows with no household size. A prediction set, not a holdout.

2.44

Mean household size across the labeled rows

Two facts shaped every decision after this

The 2,000 test rows have no household size. They are blank, not hidden, so no score can be computed on them. Evaluation happens inside the 3,027 labeled rows: a stratified 80/20 split, then ten-fold cross-validation on the 80%.

The target is censored at 4. A household of four and a household of seven look identical to the model, so a perfect prediction is impossible and an RMSE near 1 is expected rather than disappointing.

Purchase behavior

One category separates households, the rest barely move

Every feature is a summary of a respondent's whole purchase history between January 2018 and August 2024, not a single order.

Demographics

Who you are explains almost nothing

Gender, race and state are all recorded. None of them separate households in a way that a model can use.

Lower is better. Household size is measured in people, so RMSE is roughly "how many people off, on average". The three means differ by less than a single fold's standard deviation, so the ranking is not stable.

The held-out 20% agrees on the leader only

The 603 rows never used for fitting score the three learners at 1.083, 1.085 and 1.091. Random forest leads on both the folds and the holdout, but the other two swap places, which is what a 0.016 gap between models and a 0.03 standard error on the holdout predict. The holdout is a check, not a second tuning loop.

Methods

How the numbers were produced

Everything below is reproducible from R/render.R. The analysis document is model.md, and the tables the site reads are written to outputs/.

Data

The data is Open E-commerce 1.0, published by Berke et al. in Scientific Data (2024). It contains the Amazon purchase histories of 5,027 U.S. survey respondents who agreed to share them, exported from Amazon's order-history reports and linked to their survey answers by a randomly generated respondent ID. Collection ran from November 2022 to March 2023 and covers purchases back to January 2018.

Each purchase row is a line item, not an order: it has an order date but no order ID. The log has 1,850,717 line items from January 2018 to August 2024, and every survey ID appears in it. The 2,000 rows flagged test have no household size, so they are a prediction set rather than a holdout.

Berke, A., Calacci, D., Mahari, R., Yabe, T., Larson, K., & Pentland, S. (2024). Open e-commerce 1.0, five years of crowdsourced U.S. Amazon purchase histories with user demographics. Scientific Data, 11, 491. doi:10.1038/s41597-024-03329-6

Features

  • Purchase lines rolled up to one row per respondent
  • Volume: lines per day, total spend, total items, average line value and quantity
  • Three keyword bins: children's products, food and grocery, shared consumables
  • Demographics: gender and state as real factors, not numeric codes

Three decisions that shaped the model

  1. Category bins need tight rules. A loose keyword match counts pet toys as children's products, and counts pet food, storage containers and blenders as groceries. The bins here name the categories they accept.
  2. Time has to be anchored. Dividing purchase lines by days since the first order produces a different rate on every run if the end date follows the system date. The snapshot is fixed at the last order date, 2024-08-15.
  3. Categoricals stay categorical. Encoding state as as.numeric(factor(x)) tells a linear model that alphabetically distant states are numerically distant. States with fewer than 20 respondents also break lm() in cross-validation, so they are pooled into "Other".

Evaluation

  • Stratified 80/20 split of the 3,027 labeled rows, before any model is fitted
  • Ten folds inside the 80%, the same folds for all three learners
  • One holdout score on the 20%, used to confirm rather than to choose
  • RMSE in household-size units, reported per fold
  • Predictions rounded and clipped to the legal range 1–4 for every learner
  • Squared-error loss shrinks predictions toward the mean, so the predictions are almost all 2s and 3s

Limitations

What this analysis cannot claim

These are the boundaries of the result, not footnotes. Each one constrains how far the conclusion travels.

No holdout score from the test file

The 2,000 test rows are unlabeled, so the holdout comes from inside the 3,027 labeled rows: 80% for cross-validation and model selection, 20% touched once at the end. The final predictions refit the winning learner on all 3,027 labeled rows.

The target is censored at 4

Households of four and households of seven are the same label. Part of the residual error is this ceiling, not model failure, and no feature can fix it.

Keyword bins are approximations

The three category bins are hand-written regular expressions, not Amazon's taxonomy. They are documented and reproducible, but they will miss products and they carry residual miscategorization.

XGBoost was tuned on this data

Its hyperparameters come from a grid search on these same rows, so its cross-validated score is mildly optimistic relative to the untuned linear model and forest. It still does not win.

Three learners, not a leaderboard

Linear regression, a random forest and XGBoost cover the plausible shapes here: linear, piecewise-constant, and boosted. They finish within two hundredths of each other, so the comparison is a tie rather than a ranking.

Gift cards are included

The log has 41,665 gift-card lines from 3,189 respondents, about 3% of total spend. They are transfers rather than household consumption, and the source paper flags them as a known quirk. Excluding them is a reasonable robustness check that this analysis does not run.

The sample is not the U.S. population

Berke et al. report that the sample skews female, younger and more educated than the census, and under-represents Black, Other and multiracial respondents. The model describes these 5,027 volunteers, not Amazon shoppers generally.

Observational, not causal

Buying children's products correlates with a larger household. That does not mean buying them causes a larger household, or the reverse. Nothing here is a causal estimate.
